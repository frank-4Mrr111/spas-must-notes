Topic 1: Time Value of Money — Simple and Compound Interest

Objectives: By the end, you can explain time value of money, calculate simple interest, compound interest, present value, accumulated value, discount, and nominal vs effective rates.

Definition: Time value of money means money today is worth more than the same money tomorrow, because money today can earn interest. 1,000 today, at 10% for 1 year, becomes 1,100 in one year. 1,000 in one year is worth less today, because you must wait.

Why it matters: An actuary must compare money paid at different times. A premium paid today and a claim paid in 20 years are not directly comparable. You must bring them to the same time — present value or accumulated value — before you compare them.

Key words: Principal (P) = starting money. Interest (I) = extra earned. Rate (i) = interest per period, e.g. 0.10 = 10%. Time (n) = number of periods. Accumulated Value = money after interest. Present Value = money today that will grow to a future amount.

Simple Interest: Interest only on principal. I = P × i × n. Accumulated = P + I = P(1 + i×n).

Compound Interest: Interest on principal + past interest. Accumulated = P(1 + i)n. Present Value = Future ÷ (1 + i)n.

Discount: v = 11 + i. Present Value = Future × vn.

Worked Example — Simple: P = 10,000, i = 10% = 0.10, n = 3 years, simple. I = 10,000 × 0.10 × 3 = 3,000. Accumulated = 10,000 + 3,000 = 13,000.

Worked Example — Compound: P = 10,000, i = 10%, n = 3 years, compound. Accumulated = 10,000 × (1.10)3 = 10,000 × 1.331 = 13,310. Compound is higher than simple because year 2 earns interest on year 1 interest.

Worked Example — Present Value: You need 13,310 in 3 years, i = 10%. Present Value = 13,310 ÷ (1.10)3 = 13,310 ÷ 1.331 = 10,000.

Common exam mistake: Using simple formula for compound, or forgetting to convert % to decimal. 10% = 0.10, not 10. Always convert first.

Topic 2: Annuities Certain

Objectives: By the end, you can define an annuity certain, distinguish annuity-immediate and annuity-due, calculate present and accumulated values, and solve for payment, rate, or time.

Definition: An annuity certain is a series of equal payments made at regular intervals for a fixed number of periods, with no condition on life. Example: 1,000 every year for 10 years.

Why it matters: Loans, mortgages, and savings plans are annuities certain. An actuary must know their value today and in future to price them and to compare them.

Annuity-immediate: Payments at END of each period. Present value = payment × a-angle-n = payment × 1 − vni.

Annuity-due: Payments at START of each period. Present value = payment × ä-angle-n = payment × 1 − vnd, where d = i1 + i. Annuity-due is larger because first payment is immediate.

Accumulated value: Immediate: payment × s-angle-n = payment × (1 + i)n − 1i. Due: payment × s̈-angle-n = payment × (1 + i)n − 1d.

Worked Example — Immediate: Payment 1,000 at end of each year for 5 years, i = 10%. v = 0.9091, v5 = 0.6209. a-angle-5 = (1 − 0.6209) ÷ 0.10 = 3.791. Present value = 1,000 × 3.791 = 3,791.

Worked Example — Due: Same, but at start. d = 0.10 ÷ 1.10 = 0.0909. ä-angle-5 = (1 − 0.6209) ÷ 0.0909 = 4.170. Present value = 1,000 × 4.170 = 4,170. Due is higher because you get the first 1,000 now.

Common exam mistake: Using immediate formula for due, or vice versa. Always ask: first payment at start or end? Start = due, End = immediate.

Topic 3: Mortality and Life Tables

Objectives: By the end, you can explain mortality, read a life table, calculate qx, px, lx, dx, and use a life table to find probabilities of survival and death.

Definition: Mortality is the rate at which people die at a given age. A life table is a table that shows, for a group starting at age 0, how many survive to each age, how many die at each age, and the probability of dying or surviving.

Why it matters: Life insurance pays when someone dies. An actuary must know: what is the chance a 30-year-old dies this year? In 10 years? The life table answers that. Without it, you cannot price life insurance.

Symbols: x = age. lx = number alive at age x. dx = number dying between x and x+1 = lx − lx+1. qx = probability a person aged x dies within 1 year = dxlx. px = probability a person aged x survives 1 year = 1 − qx = lx+1lx.

n-year probabilities: npx = lx+nlx. nqx = 1 − npx.

Worked Example: Life table: l30 = 100,000, l31 = 99,800, l40 = 97,000. Then d30 = 100,000 − 99,800 = 200. q30 = 200 ÷ 100,000 = 0.002. p30 = 1 − 0.002 = 0.998. 10p30 = 97,000 ÷ 100,000 = 0.97. So a 30-year-old has 97% chance to reach 40, 3% chance to die before 40.

Common exam mistake: Confusing qx with dx. dx is a count (200 people). qx is a probability (0.002). Always divide by lx to get probability.

Topic 4: Life Insurance — Whole Life, Term, Endowment

Objectives: By the end, you can define life insurance, explain whole life, term, endowment, calculate their single premiums (APV) using life table and discount, and compare them.

Definition: Life insurance pays a sum assured when the insured dies (or survives, for endowment). The actuary calculates the Actuarial Present Value (APV) — the expected present value of the payment, allowing for both interest and mortality.

Why it matters: The insurer must charge enough to pay claims, but not so much that no one buys. APV is the fair price before expenses and profit.

Whole Life: Pays whenever you die. APV = sum × Ax, where Ax = sum of (probability of dying each year × discount).

Term Insurance: Pays only if you die within n years. If you survive n years, it pays nothing. APV = sum × A1x:n. Cheaper than whole life because many survive.

Endowment: Pays if you die within n years OR if you survive n years. APV = sum × Ax:n = term + pure endowment. Most expensive because it always pays.

Pure Endowment: Pays only if you survive n years. APV = sum × nEx = sum × vn × npx.

Worked Example — Term: Age 30, sum 100,000, term 10 years, A130:10 = 0.03. APV = 100,000 × 0.03 = 3,000. That is the single premium (before expenses) for 10-year term cover.

Worked Example — Pure Endowment: Age 30, sum 100,000, 20 years, v20 = 0.1486, 20p30 = 0.97. 20E30 = 0.1486 × 0.97 = 0.1441. APV = 100,000 × 0.1441 = 14,410.

Common exam mistake: Using whole life APV for term, or forgetting discount. APV always includes BOTH mortality and discount. If you forget discount, your answer is too high.

Topic 5: Life Annuities

Objectives: By the end, you can define a life annuity, distinguish whole life, temporary, and deferred annuities, calculate their APV, and explain the difference from annuities certain.

Definition: A life annuity pays a regular amount while the person is alive. It stops when they die. Unlike annuity certain, it is not guaranteed for a fixed number of years — it depends on survival.

Why it matters: Pensions are life annuities. A retired person needs income for as long as they live, not for a fixed 10 years. The actuary must price that, allowing for the chance the person lives long.

Whole Life Annuity-due: Pays at start of each year while alive. APV = payment × äx.

Temporary Life Annuity: Pays while alive, but only for n years max. APV = payment × äx:n.

Deferred Life Annuity: Payments start after m years, if alive then. APV = payment × m|äx = payment × vm × mpx × äx+m.

Difference from annuity certain: Certain pays even if you die. Life annuity stops at death, so it is cheaper for same payment.

Worked Example — Whole Life: Age 60, payment 10,000 per year, due, ä60 = 12.0. APV = 10,000 × 12.0 = 120,000. That is the single premium to buy a pension of 10,000 per year for life from age 60.

Worked Example — Temporary: Age 60, payment 10,000 per year, due, for 10 years max, ä60:10 = 7.5. APV = 10,000 × 7.5 = 75,000. Cheaper than whole life because payments stop after 10 years even if alive.

Common exam mistake: Using annuity certain formula for life annuity. Life annuity must use life table probabilities — you cannot ignore mortality.

Topic 6: Net Premiums and Reserves

Objectives: By the end, you can explain net premium, use equivalence principle, calculate annual net premium for insurance and annuities, and calculate prospective reserve.

Definition: Net premium is the premium that exactly covers expected claims, with no expenses and no profit. It is found by Equivalence Principle: APV of premiums = APV of benefits.

Why it matters: If premium is too low, insurer cannot pay claims. If too high, no one buys. Net premium is the fair base. Gross premium = net premium + loading for expenses and profit.

Equivalence: Annual net premium P × APV of premium annuity = APV of benefit. So P = APV of benefitAPV of premium annuity.

Example — Whole life: Benefit APV = sum × Ax. Premium annuity = äx (if premiums paid for life, due). So P = sum × Ax ÷ äx.

Prospective Reserve: Reserve at time t = APV of future benefits − APV of future net premiums. It is money the insurer must hold now to meet future claims, after allowing for future premiums still to come.

Worked Example — Net Premium: Age 30, sum 100,000 whole life, A30 = 0.20, ä30 = 15.0. APV benefit = 100,000 × 0.20 = 20,000. P = 20,000 ÷ 15.0 = 1,333.33 per year. That is the annual net premium, paid for life, due.

Worked Example — Reserve: At time 10, APV future benefits = 25,000, APV future premiums = 10,000. Reserve = 25,000 − 10,000 = 15,000. The insurer must hold 15,000 now for this policy.

Common exam mistake: Forgetting that premiums also have APV. You cannot divide benefit APV by number of years. You must divide by APV of premium annuity, which allows for discount and survival.
